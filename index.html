the merge

I was trained to read cells. I learned to read landscapes. I never stopped reading the self.

Most people pick a lane. I noticed the lanes were the same road. A genome is a map. A river is a sentence. A sutra is a dataset four thousand years deep. So I stopped switching fields and started collapsing them — biotech, geoinformatics and the search for truth folded into a single way of looking. That fold is this website.

// the 93 × Geospatial Science — Full Essay

The Map Is Not the Territory —
Osho, Korzybski, and the Limits of GIS

6 min read  ·  Truth × Earth Science

We build satellite maps of the entire earth — every river bend, every deforested patch, every shifting coastline — with millimetre precision. Terabytes of data. Algorithms that can detect a single burned field from 700 kilometres above.

And yet, the 93 kept saying something that disturbs me every time I open QGIS:

"The map is not the territory. The word 'water' will not quench your thirst. The word 'fire' will not burn your hand. All our knowledge is about maps — and we have completely forgotten the territory."

— the 93

He was borrowing from Alfred Korzybski — a Polish-American philosopher who coined the phrase in 1931. But the 93 took it further. He wasn't just talking about language. He was talking about the entire project of human knowledge — science included.

What GIS Actually Does

Geospatial Information Science is, at its core, the art of making maps. We take the living, breathing, chaotic reality of the earth and convert it into layers — raster layers, vector layers, spectral indices, classified polygons. We assign a number to a forest. We draw a boundary around a river. We give coordinates to a village.

This is extraordinarily useful. NDVI tells us how much of a forest is dying. Flood inundation maps save thousands of lives. Land use classification tracks deforestation faster than any ground survey ever could.

But here is what GIS cannot do: it cannot capture what it feels like to stand inside that forest.

The 0.73 NDVI value of a tree does not contain the sound of rain on its leaves. The polygon boundary of the Brahmaputra does not hold the terror of a flood. The land cover class "cropland" does not know the name of the farmer who planted it, or the prayer she said before sowing.

the 93's Radical Point

the 93 wasn't anti-science. He was deeply read in physics, biology, psychology. What he was against was something subtler — the confusion of the representation with the reality.

When a GIS analyst looks at a deforestation map and says "the forest is 40% degraded" — they are right. The data says so. But they are also missing something enormous: the forest doesn't know it's 40% degraded. The forest is just the forest — complete, present, indifferent to our classifications.

"Science is great — but it only touches the circumference of existence. The centre remains untouched, unknown. And that centre is you."

— the 93

The "centre" the 93 talks about — I think of it as the un-mappable. The thing that exists before you put a coordinate on it.

The Heisenberg Connection

Physics stumbled into this same wall in 1927. Werner Heisenberg proved that the act of observing a particle changes its behaviour — you cannot know both the position and the momentum of an electron simultaneously. The measurement disturbs the measured.

Remote sensing has its own version of this. When we fly a UAV over a wildlife corridor to map animal movement, the UAV disturbs the very animals we're mapping. When we classify a community's land use from satellite imagery, we impose categories that may not reflect how that community actually uses or understands the land.

The observer changes the observed. In quantum physics. In ecology. In GIS. And in the 93's meditation halls.

So What Do We Do With This?

I'm not suggesting we stop making maps. Maps are among the most powerful tools humanity has ever built. Climate science, disaster response, biodiversity conservation — none of it is possible without spatial data.

But the 93's point — and Korzybski's — is a call for humility. The map is a tool, not a truth. The GIS layer is a reduction, not a revelation. The satellite image is a single wavelength of a reality that exists in infinite dimensions.

The best geospatial scientists I've read about — the ones doing genuinely groundbreaking work — always combine their spatial analysis with field visits, community interviews, indigenous knowledge systems. They know that the truth exists somewhere between the raster cell and the lived experience.

That gap — between the coordinate and the place — is where the real work happens.

"Go beyond the map. The territory is waiting."
